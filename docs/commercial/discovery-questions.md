# Discovery call framework

Ten questions designed to test whether demand is real. Several are written
to invite a "no", because a question that cannot produce a negative answer
produces no information.

## How to run it

**Ask before you demo.** Once you have shown the product, every answer is
contaminated by politeness and by the vocabulary you just taught them.
Fifteen minutes of questions, then fifteen of demo, is a better call than
thirty of demo.

**Ask for the last instance, not the general case.** "How do you usually
handle X" gets you their idealised process. "Walk me through the last time
X happened" gets you the truth.

**Write down their words.** Their vocabulary for their own workflow is worth
more than your feature names, and it belongs in the next version of the
outreach email.

**Watch for these three signals** more than anything they say:

1. They describe a workaround in detail. People only build workarounds for
   problems that cost them something.
2. They name a specific instance, with a date or a security. Specificity
   means it actually happened.
3. They ask who else sees it, or what compliance would say. That is a buying
   question disguised as an objection.

---

## 1. Existing research workflow

> Walk me through the last name you took from a first look to a position.
> Where did the written work actually live at each step?

**Testing:** whether there is a process at all, and how fragmented it is.

**A weak answer** is a description of an ideal process. **A strong answer**
names tools, and usually names three or four.

**Follow up with:** "And if I asked you for the version of that note the PM
actually read, could you get it?"

---

## 2. Idea generation and triage

> How many names are in flight right now, and how do you decide which ones
> get your time this week?

**Testing:** whether a pipeline exists as a concept. A firm with no notion
of "in flight" has no use for an idea pipeline.

**Listen for:** a spreadsheet. A spreadsheet is both the competitor and the
evidence of need.

---

## 3. Recommendation communication

> When an analyst wants you to buy something, what literally arrives, and in
> what?

**Testing:** whether the recommendation is an artifact or a conversation.
Tesseract's frozen-reasoning model only matters if there is something being
submitted.

**If the answer is "they just tell me":** that is still a fit, but the pitch
is different. It is about creating a record where none exists, which is a
harder sell than improving one that does.

---

## 4. PM decision process

> Think of the last recommendation you turned down. What happened to it?

**The single most informative question in this list.** It tests the exact
gap Tesseract is built around.

**Almost always:** nothing happened to it. It evaporated. That is the
opening.

**Follow up with:** "If the analyst asked you in six months why you passed,
what would you look at?" If the honest answer is "my memory", say so back to
them and stop talking.

---

## 5. Documenting disagreement

> When you and an analyst disagree on a name, is that recorded anywhere?

**Testing:** whether disagreement is treated as information or as friction.

**Why it matters:** the firms that get the most from a decision record are
the ones that already believe a documented disagreement is worth keeping.
A firm that treats disagreement as something to smooth over will not adopt
this no matter how good it is.

**This question can disqualify a prospect, and that is useful.**

---

## 6. Tracking decisions over time

> Could you produce a list of every decision your team made on a given
> security over the last two years, with the reasoning behind each one? How
> long would it take?

**Testing:** the cost of the current state, in their own estimate.

**Listen for the unit.** "A couple of hours" means it is possible and
annoying. "I could not" means it is impossible, which is a much stronger
position for you. "Why would I need that?" means they are not a fit, and you
should take that seriously rather than explaining why they should want it.

---

## 7. Outcome reviews

> Do you do post-mortems on investment decisions? Walk me through the last
> one.

**Testing:** whether review is a practice or an aspiration.

**Nearly everyone says they do.** Ask for the last one. The gap between the
answer and the example is the real finding.

**Follow up with:** "When you review a decision, do you compare it against
the thesis as it was written at the time, or against the thesis as it reads
now?" Most people have never distinguished the two, and the moment they do
is usually when they understand the product.

---

## 8. Organizational memory

> If a senior analyst left tomorrow, what would you lose that is not written
> down?

**Testing:** whether the institutional-memory argument lands emotionally.
This is the question that turns an interesting product into an urgent one.

**For a firm that has actually been through this**, the answer is immediate
and specific. Those are your best prospects.

---

## 9. Adoption friction

> Who on your team would refuse to use this, and why?

**Deliberately asks for the objection** rather than for enthusiasm. Nobody
answers "no one", and the answer tells you where the product fails.

**Follow up with:** "What does your team currently get asked to do that
nobody actually does?" That reveals their tolerance for process, which is
the single largest adoption risk for a system that depends on analysts
writing things down.

---

## 10. Budget ownership and willingness to pay

> If you decided this was worth having, whose budget would it come out of,
> and what would you compare the cost to?

**Two questions in one, both necessary.** The first tells you whether you
are talking to a buyer or an influencer. The second tells you the reference
price, which you will never guess correctly.

**Then the real test:**

> If this existed exactly as you would want it in six months, is it
> something you would pay for, or something you would like to have?

**Ask it plainly and let the silence sit.** A hesitation is the answer. Note
it as a hesitation rather than rounding it up to a yes, because the whole
point of this exercise is to find out, and a false positive here costs
months.

---

## After the call

Write up, the same day:

- **Their words** for the problem. Verbatim.
- **The specific instance** they described, if they gave one.
- **The objection** from question 9.
- **The budget answer** from question 10, including hesitations.
- **Your honest read:** real pain, polite interest, or no fit. Record "no
  fit" when it is "no fit". A pipeline of polite interest is how a founder
  spends a year learning nothing.

Then use [outreach-templates.md](outreach-templates.md), template 3.
