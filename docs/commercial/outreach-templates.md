# Outreach templates

Four templates. All of them are short on purpose: a PM reads email on a
phone between meetings, and a long first email from an unknown sender does
not get read at all.

**Rules that apply to all four.**

- One ask per email, and it is always a short call, never a document.
- No claim that is not in [product-overview.md](product-overview.md).
- No customer references, because there are none.
- Do not say "revolutionary", "AI-powered", "game-changing", or "disrupt".
- Do not attach anything to a first email.
- Replace every `[bracket]` before sending. An unreplaced bracket has
  ended more outreach than a bad pitch.

---

## 1. Warm introduction, via an existing contact

Sent to the person who can make the introduction, not to the target. The
goal is a forwardable paragraph.

> **Subject:** Quick intro ask: [target firm]
>
> Hi [name],
>
> I have been building something for the research and decision side of
> investment teams and I am looking for a few people to tell me where it
> breaks.
>
> It is a system of record for investment decisions. The reasoning behind a
> recommendation gets frozen when it is submitted, the PM's decision is
> captured against it, and the whole thing is reviewable a year later. The
> problem I am going after is that most teams cannot reconstruct why they
> did something six months ago.
>
> Would you be willing to introduce me to [person] at [firm]? Twenty
> minutes, and I am after criticism rather than a sale. Happy to send
> something forwardable if that is easier.
>
> [Your name]

**Why it is shaped this way.** It gives the connector a one-sentence
description they can repeat, and it lowers the social cost by asking for
criticism rather than a meeting about buying something.

## 2. Direct introduction, to a PM or fund founder

Cold. Assume eight seconds of attention and no prior context.

> **Subject:** The reasoning behind your decisions from last year
>
> Hi [name],
>
> A question rather than a pitch: when your team revisits a position from
> last year, where does the original reasoning come from? Not the current
> thesis, the reasoning as it stood when the decision was made.
>
> I am building Tesseract, which keeps that record. An analyst's
> recommendation is frozen at submission, the PM's decision and rationale
> attach to it, and the review afterwards lives in the same place. Research,
> recommendation, decision and review as linked records rather than four
> folders.
>
> It is early. I am not selling you anything yet. I am looking for three or
> four teams to tell me whether this matches how a desk actually works.
>
> Worth twenty minutes?
>
> [Your name]
> [link to the landing page]

**Variant for a smaller fund**, where the founder is also the PM: replace
"your team" with "you" throughout and cut the second paragraph's last
sentence. The linked-records argument matters less to a two-person shop than
"you will remember why you did this".

## 3. Follow-up after a demonstration

Send within 24 hours. The purpose is to make their internal conversation
easy, not to re-pitch.

> **Subject:** Following up on Tesseract
>
> Hi [name],
>
> Thanks for the time. Two things you said that I am taking away:
>
> 1. [specific thing they said about their process]
> 2. [specific friction or objection they raised]
>
> On [the objection]: [one or two honest sentences. If it is a real gap, say
> it is a real gap and say when it is addressed. Do not argue.]
>
> One page on what it is and what it is not: [attach product-overview].
>
> If it is useful, the next step I would suggest is a scoped evaluation:
> [two or three] of your people, one sector, four to six weeks, with us
> doing the setup. I have written up what that involves if you want to see
> it before committing to anything.
>
> Either way, the two points above were worth the call. Thank you.
>
> [Your name]

**Why the two takeaways come first.** It proves you listened, and it gives
them language to describe the conversation to a colleague. Most follow-ups
fail because they are about the sender.

## 4. Follow-up expressing design-partner interest

For a firm that has shown real interest and now needs to know what they are
agreeing to.

> **Subject:** Design partnership: what it would involve
>
> Hi [name],
>
> You asked what being a design partner actually means. Here is the honest
> version.
>
> **What you would get:** a scoped evaluation with us doing the setup,
> direct input into what gets built next, your recommendation and decision
> process modelled properly rather than approximated, and preferential terms
> when there is a commercial release.
>
> **What I would need:** [two or three] named users, one real workflow
> rather than a sandbox, and an hour every couple of weeks for honest
> feedback.
>
> **What is not ready, so you are not surprised:** market-data licensing for
> external users is an open dependency, the AI assistant currently runs on
> your own provider key, and this is a pre-release platform rather than a
> production deployment.
>
> Nothing here is binding. If after the evaluation it is not useful, that is
> a result I need too.
>
> The full framework is attached if you want to take it to [compliance / your
> COO / your partner].
>
> [Your name]

**Attach** [design-partner-framework.md](design-partner-framework.md).

**Why the limitations are in the email.** A firm that discovers the
market-data dependency during a security review will treat it as something
you concealed. A firm that was told in writing treats it as a known
condition. The same fact, with opposite consequences.

## What not to send

- A deck as a first touch. The landing page does that job and costs them
  nothing.
- A free trial offer. There is no self-serve product and an unsupported
  trial of a pre-release platform produces a bad impression you cannot
  recover.
- Any number about returns, time saved, or efficiency. You have no data and
  a PM will ask where it came from.
- A second follow-up inside a week. One follow-up, then leave it two weeks,
  then one more. Then stop.
